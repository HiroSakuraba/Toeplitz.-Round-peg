# v350. The disk bound for all inward tilts

The polygon has vertices $(0,0),(W,0),(W,b),(a,b),(c,H),(0,H)$ with $0<c<a<W$ and $0<b<H$. Moving left or down from any point of the filled polygon stays inside until an axis is reached. Call this the downward-closure property. The inward wall may be arbitrarily steep.

**Theorem.** Every strict side cutoff below $\sqrt2 r$ survives all sufficiently small registered Jordan redraws, where $r$ is the reference inradius. Together with [v345](v344-v345-convex-and-L-disk-bounds.md) and [v349](v349-all-outward-tilted-L.md) this covers every direction of tilt for this six-edge model.

## Selecting a square on a monotone frontier

The following argument also works for a simple arc from $(W,0)$ to $(0,H)$ on which $x$ never increases and $y$ never decreases, including horizontal and vertical pieces. Assume the arc and the two axes enclose a Jordan region. Let $b$ be the height of its initial vertical segment and $c$ the length of its final horizontal segment (zero if absent). First suppose $b<W$ and $c<H$.

Mark two adjacent square corners on the axes as $(u,0)$ and $(0,v)$. Their completions in counterclockwise order are $(t,u)$ and $(v,t)$, with $t=u+v$. To put these last two points on the frontier define $A(t)$ to be the interval of its ordinates at $x=t$ and $B(t)$ the interval of its abscissas at $y=t$. Choose
$$
 t\in A(t)+B(t),\qquad u\in A(t)\cap(t-B(t)),\qquad v=t-u.
$$
Each section is a closed interval, possibly a singleton. For increasing $t$, every element of either later section is at most every element of its earlier section. Thus at most one span $t$ works. At zero the sum is $H+W$. At $m=\min(W,H)$ the minimum sum is at most $m$: if $m=W<H$, $A(W)$ contains zero and $B(W)$ is bounded by $W$; interchange roles for the height cap. Both minima vanish for a double cap. The one-sided limits of each section at a jump are its two endpoints, by continuity and monotonicity of the arc (at the outer cap use the inward limit). Hence the decreasing interval sum crosses the increasing diagonal, including at jumps. The selected $u$ form a compact interval. Neither anchor is zero: $u=0$ requires $t=W=v$ and the frontier to contain $(W,W)$, contrary to $b<W$. Likewise $v=0$ would force $c\ge H$. Both anchors are therefore interior to their axis edges and both frontier contacts are interior to the arc.

**Degree.** In these four marked parameter arcs the displayed formulas describe the entire zero set of the square residual. It is compact and lies away from all arc endpoints; the two frontier marks are distinct and appear in order since $0<u,v<t$. Choose a neighborhood of the family with zero-free boundary. Approximate the frontier, in its given parametrization, by smooth parametrized arcs with $x'<0$ and $y'>0$ and the same endpoints. For instance, apply Bernstein polynomials separately to the monotone coordinate functions on $[0,1]$, then mix in a vanishing positive fraction of the straight chord. The coefficient differences preserve monotonicity, and the chord gives strict derivative signs. Leave the axis edges fixed.

Each approximant has one marked solution: its sections are single-valued and strictly decreasing. It belongs to the neighborhood for all sufficiently close approximants by compactness, since any limiting solution on the closed marking arcs satisfies the same section equations (the span cannot tend to zero, since the frontier avoids the origin). At the solution, write the two frontier conditions as $u=A(u+v)$ and $v=B(u+v)$ using the smooth section functions. The determinant in the anchor coordinates is $1-A'-B'>0$. The two remaining edge parameters are recovered at nonzero coordinate speed, so the full four-coordinate residual has nonsingular derivative. Its degree has absolute value one. Uniform convergence preserves that degree on the isolating neighborhood, then on every sufficiently close registered Jordan redraw. Shrinking the neighborhood proves survival above every strict cutoff below the minimum side of the original family, as in [v345](v344-v345-convex-and-L-disk-bounds.md).

**Why this is large enough.** Move a disk of radius $r$ horizontally and vertically until its center is $(r,r)$. Each translated point is coordinatewise below its original position and still in the first quadrant. Downward closure therefore keeps the entire disk inside. In particular the filled region contains $(2r,r)$ and $(r,2r)$. At any $t<2r$, every ordinate in $A(t)$ and every abscissa in $B(t)$ is at least $r$, by monotonicity (at $2r$ use the upper endpoint of each section). Their sum would be at least $2r>t$. The selected span thus satisfies $t\ge2r$. Every selected square has side
$$
\sqrt{u^2+v^2}\ \ge\ (u+v)/\sqrt2\ \ge\ \sqrt2 r .
$$
The bounding box of the square has width $t$; the disk argument compares this width directly with the disk diameter. It applies even when both upper contacts lie on the same inclined edge.

## End contacts for the six-edge model

For $b\ge W$, [v348](v348-thick-lower-arms.md) applies directly. For $c>H$, both horizontal supports from the left closing edge extend farther than $H$. The horizontally sliding side-$H$ squares have odd degree by the strip argument in [v349](v349-all-outward-tilted-L.md), which uses only the strip, the two support lengths and the straight left closure. Their side reaches the disk target since $2r\le H$.

For $c=H$, the sliding interval shrinks to the side-$H$ square at the left end. To verify its degree, increase $c$ slightly, keeping $c<a$ and all other lengths fixed. Near that square write the lower-left contact as $(x,y)$ with $x,y\ge0$, $xy=0$. The lower-right contact lies strictly inside the bottom, so the first side vector is $(d,-y)$ with $d$ near $H$. The upper-left and upper-right vertices are $(x+y,y+d)$ and $(x+d+y,d)$. The upper-left contact lies on the left or the top. For $y>0$ it is on the top and $x=0$, so the upper-right vertex is $(H,H-y)$. This cannot touch the slope, whose abscissa below the top is strictly greater than $c\ge H$. For $y=0$ and $x>0$ the top forces $d=H$, giving exactly the sliding squares $0<x\le c-H$. For $x=y=0$ the upper-right contact $(d,d)$ similarly forces $d=H$. These exhaust the nearby edge choices including corners. There is therefore a common isolating neighborhood through the small change of $c$; the odd degree for $c>H$ transfers to the singleton at $c=H$. This finishes the end cases.
